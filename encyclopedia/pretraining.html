Pretraining and fine-tuning

Pretraining teaches a transformer from a huge pile of text by predicting missing or next pieces, and fine-tuning continues that training on a smaller, specific job. BERT fills blanks, GPT-style models continue text, and instruction tuning is a later cousin that teaches the model to treat a user's message as a request.

There are two lessons, and mixing them up is how beginners get lost in product pages. The first lesson is pretraining. You take a huge pile of text, much larger than any labeled set you could afford, and you invent a question the text can answer by itself. Hide a piece and guess it, or guess the next piece. You do this for so long that the weights become a compressed habit of the pile. The second lesson is fine-tuning. You take those weights, already literate in a statistical sense, and you keep training on a smaller set that has the actual job: is this review positive, does this sentence answer this question, which label belongs on this paragraph. The second lesson is cheaper because the first one did the broad work. I want the order to feel like apprenticeship. First the long, dumb, enormous practice. Then the short, specific correction. People say 'the model' as if it arrived knowing their task. It arrived knowing the pile. The task was a later, smaller class.

BERT, from Jacob Devlin and colleagues in 2018, is the blank-filling version that a lot of labs met first. You mask some words and ask the transformer to restore them, and because the mask sits in the middle, the model is allowed to look in both directions. That bidirectionality suited jobs that are more like judging a whole sentence than like writing the next one: classification, tagging, 'does this passage support this claim.' The original setup also trained a next-sentence objective, a guess about whether two spans followed each other. The famous part is the blanks. Follow-on work showed you can do without the extra sentence objective and still get a strong model, which is a useful reminder not to marry every auxiliary task in the first paper. BERT is a pretrained model you adapt. On its own, filling blanks is not a conversation. It is a very good student of what tends to occupy a hole in a sentence, and of how to be fine-tuned into a judge.

GPT-style models take the other bet, and they take it with the same transformer backbone. The line starts in 2018 with Alec Radford and colleagues: train the model to continue text, left to right, so each piece is predicted from the pieces before it. The model never sees the future of the sentence during the guess. That sounds weaker than BERT's both-directions view, and for some judging tasks it was. For drafting, it is the natural exam, because drafting is continuation. A quiet discovery of that line is that many tasks can be phrased as text to continue. You write a prompt that looks like the start of a worked example, and the continuation is the answer. You can even put a few examples in the prompt and steer the style without changing the weights. That trick becomes famous a little later, at larger scale. The seed is already in the pretraining choice. Continue the text. If the text looks like a task, the continuation looks like doing the task. Continuation is not a sworn statement.

Fine-tuning is the smaller lesson, and it deserves respect without being mystified. You have a few thousand, or a few hundred thousand, labeled examples of the job. You keep training, usually gently, so the pretrained habits bend toward the labels. A sentiment model, a document router, a company-specific categorizer: these are fine-tunes. The risk is as plain as the benefit. A small lesson can overwrite a broad one. Show the model only your quirks and it will become a specialist who has forgotten how sentences work outside your folder. Show it too little and it will barely move. The art, which I will not turn into a recipe with knobs, is to let the small lesson land without sanding off the literacy. For you as a beginner, the practical distinction is enough. Pretraining is someone else's enormous bill, already paid, stored in the weights. Fine-tuning is a further bill you might pay, or rent, when you have examples of one job and a reason to specialize.

Instruction tuning is the cousin, not a synonym, and the cousin is why chat products started to feel obedient. Fine-tuning on a single dataset teaches one job. Instruction tuning teaches the social shape of many jobs by example: here is a request, here is a suitable response, repeated across phrasings. 'Summarize this.' 'Extract the date.' 'Refuse if the question does not say.' The model learns that the user's text is an ask, not the first line of an article it should autocomplete in a weird direction. Researchers made this a standard stage in the early 2020s, with several public recipes aimed at the same idea. I am not going to list them like a catalog. I am going to locate the cousin on the family tree. It is still training on examples. The examples are requests and replies rather than unlabeled books or a single label set. It does not install truth. It installs a format. A model can be brilliantly pretrained and still ignore your instruction until this lesson, or a preference lesson, teaches it the format of compliance.

Lay the curriculum out as a path you can walk into the next node. Pile of text, then a self-invented exam, either blanks or continuation. That is pretraining, and BERT and GPT are the two famous answers to which exam. Then a smaller labeled lesson if you have one. That is fine-tuning. Then, if you want the model to treat a stranger's message as a task, a cousin lesson on instructions. None of these steps changes the underlying act, which is still a transformer predicting pieces. They change what the pieces are asked to be. The large language model, next, is what the continuation exam looks like when you refuse to stop scaling it. The chat window, after that, is what happens when instruction-shaped training meets a text box the public can open. If you can keep the lessons distinct, you will not be sold a fine-tune as if it were a new species, or a chat product as if the pile of text had never happened.

Why this matters

Split any model announcement you see this week into lessons. Was there a huge unlabeled pile, a smaller labeled job, or a set of requests and replies? Those are pretraining, fine-tuning, and instruction tuning, and the ad will blur them. Read a short account of BERT's blank-filling and of the 2018 GPT continuation setup, and say which one is a judge and which one is a drafter. Keep the cousin distinct: following an ask is a format, not a fact-checker. The next node scales the drafter until people start calling it large.
