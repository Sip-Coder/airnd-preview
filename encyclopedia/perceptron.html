Perceptrons

A perceptron is a single-layer learner that weighs its inputs, adds them, and thresholds the total, so the decision is one straight cut through the examples. Frank Rosenblatt presented it in 1958 as a machine that could learn categories from those examples.

I want you to see the perceptron before you see the cartoon of it. In 1958 Frank Rosenblatt, working at the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory, described a machine that learns a category. You show it examples. Each input, a pixel or some other measurement, has a weight. The machine adds the weighted inputs and compares the total with a threshold. Above the line, one class. Below it, the other. That is the whole trick, and it is a real trick. A single layer draws a cut through the space of examples. On a problem where one straight cut is enough, the weights can be nudged until the cut sits in a useful place. Rosenblatt also had hardware, the Mark I Perceptron, built around a camera and a bank of motors that adjusted weights. It was not a metaphor in a grant proposal. It was a device that got less bad at a sorting task by changing numbers. Hold that picture. Numbers, a sum, a threshold, a cut.

The cut is also the fence. If the examples you care about can be separated by one straight boundary, a perceptron has a chance. People later called those problems linearly separable, which is a formal way of saying one cut will do. Plenty of useful sorting looks like that if you have already done the hard work of choosing what to measure. The classroom wreck is exclusive-or. Take two bits. You want 'yes' when they differ and 'no' when they match. Draw the four cases as dots on a napkin: the two mixed cases sit on one diagonal, the two matching cases on the other. No single straight line puts the yeses on one side and the nos on the other. You can feel the failure without a textbook. A stack of cuts could outline the shape. One cut cannot. The geometry is not a vibe. It is a limit you can see.

In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published Perceptrons, and the book took that kind of limit seriously. They were not tourists taking a swipe at a fad. They defined a simple perceptron carefully and proved what it could not compute. Exclusive-or became the example everyone teaches because four dots fit on a board. The book goes further than the classroom sketch. It shows that some patterns people hoped were within easy reach of a single layer, including certain questions about how a figure is connected, are not available to that machine. The tone is mathematical, and the mathematics landed. If you only own one layer of weighted sums and a threshold, there are patterns you will never represent, no matter how long you turn the knobs. I want you to respect that result. A fair teacher does not rescue a method by pretending a proof was rude.

The unfair part is what people did with the result afterward. The book was widely received as a funeral for neural nets as such. That is a bigger claim than the theorems. A network with extra layers can represent patterns a single cut cannot, exclusive-or included. Minsky and Papert knew the power grew when you added layers. What they also made plain, and what was true at the time, is that nobody had a reliable general way to set the weights in those middle layers. Representing a function and learning it from examples are different jobs. The book closed the case on the simple perceptron more than it closed the case on every possible net. Readers, funders, and graduate students are allowed to be tired. Many of them walked away as if the whole family of ideas had been refuted. The chill that followed was real. The obituary was too wide.

I teach this node because you will meet the same move for the rest of your life with tools. A demo fails a simple test, and the room splits into believers who ignore the test and skeptics who bury the entire approach. The third habit is the one I want you to practice. Read the fence. Inside the fence, a perceptron is a clean sorting rule, still worth understanding because every later network is built from weighted sums and nonlinearities that a single perceptron already hints at. Outside the fence, you need more structure and a training story for that structure. Rosenblatt's machine is not a mascot and it is not a joke. It is an honest geometry. When a modern system is described to you as if it 'just understands,' picture the cut first. Then ask how many cuts, and who was allowed to move them.

Keep the dates in the order they happened. The hopeful machine is 1958. The careful book is 1969. The training method that lets many layers share the blame is not in this node, and it was not a public craft in 1969. That gap is the whole plot of the next few years in this module. People sometimes tell it as a melodrama, with villains crushing a genius. I do not buy the melodrama. I buy a simpler story. A simple learner was shown its real limits. The next learner needed a way to train hidden layers, and that way took time to become something a lab could use. If you remember one sentence from this node, remember the fair one. The book cooled the simple perceptron. It did not prove that every neural net was useless.

Why this matters

This matters because later networks are still built from weighted sums and thresholds. The romance is optional. The geometry is not. Draw the four exclusive-or dots and try to separate them with one straight cut. You cannot, and that failure is the lesson. Then look up Rosenblatt's 1958 perceptron and Minsky and Papert's book, and read the book as a limit on a simple machine rather than a curse on the whole family. When the napkin sketch is steady, you are ready for the training trick that lets more than one layer share the blame for a mistake.
