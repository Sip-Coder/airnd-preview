What a model is

A model is a bundle of learned numbers and the procedure that runs them. The chat window is a way to talk to one run of that bundle.

People say the model the way they say the intern. Drop that picture before it costs you. A model is a saved result. Training, the expensive part, already happened before you opened the tab. What got saved is a gigantic pile of numbers, plus a procedure that knows how to turn your text into a guess by pushing it through those numbers. The chat window is not the model. The window is a counter, a text box, and a set of product decisions wrapped around one run. When you type, you are steering that run. You are not, in any ordinary chat, sitting down beside the training job and editing the file for the whole world. The file is large, dull, and indifferent to your mood. You rent a run of it the way you rent a machine for an afternoon, except the machine's only motion is a guess.

I want the two words to stay distinct. The procedure is the shape of the machine: how pieces move, how they look back at earlier pieces, how a next piece gets chosen. The numbers are the habit that procedure learned. Change the numbers and the habit changes, even if the procedure looks the same on a diagram. Two menu items can share a brand and still be different habits, because the numbers differ, or because the hidden instructions wrapped around them differ. You do not need a tour of every logo on the market. You need to know that a name on a button is a product, and the product is renting you a particular guess. Ask what happens on a wrong answer, where the text goes, and how you would notice a silent change. Those three questions tell you more than a parameter count shouted on a slide.

Here is the part that frees you. A run does not have feelings about being restarted. If the draft is perfumed, close it and say the job again, shorter, with the limit you forgot. If you were vague, the model did not get its feelings hurt. It completed a vague job in a vague way. Starting over is a craft move, the same way you crumple a bad opening line. Some products do store notes about you between sessions, and some say they will train on what you typed. Those are extra behaviors you should read about in the settings. They are not the definition of a model. The definition is the file and the procedure. Memory features are furniture in the room around the file. If a toggle says chats may be used to improve the model, believe that your text might become later training material, on their terms, not yours.

There is a subtlety I will not let turn into a myth. During a single run, the model can follow a pattern you put in the message. Paste two of your old notes, ask for a third in that shape, and the draft leans toward your shape. That lean is real, and it is useful. It is also temporary. The numbers in the file did not become your numbers. The pattern is available only while it fits in this pass. Start a fresh thread and forget to bring an example, and you are back to the average habit. I like this, once you see it. You stop hoping the tool remembers your business, and you start keeping a small set of examples you can paste on purpose. Three scrubbed notes from your own work will beat a paragraph of adjectives about your brand voice.

Size is the question beginners ask next, and I will answer it only as far as the bill and the job. A larger model often handles a messier request with fewer stumbles, and it often costs more and waits longer. A smaller one can be the right tool for a narrow chore you run all day, especially if the chore is well specified and you check it. Local models, the ones that run on a machine you control, change the privacy trade, and they are still models: a habit, a procedure, a guess. None of these choices turn the guess into a colleague who has met your client. Pick by the job, the cost, and where the text goes. Leave the spec-sheet contests to people who enjoy them. Write the choice in one line so you can defend it without reciting a leaderboard.

When we build later, this picture keeps the work small enough to finish. You are not training a mind. You are writing a brief, choosing examples, and deciding what a run is allowed to touch. If a vendor talks as if you are onboarding a hire, translate it back. You are opening a file of tendencies and asking for one continuation. The next node is about the kind of continuation that feels like a break from older software: the draft that was not sitting in a database waiting for you, the thing people mean by generative. Bring a real chore to that picture, not a hypothetical empire. The chore that repeats, with a check you can do, is the one worth a brief. A model that drafts it is a tool. A model that is supposed to become your whole company is a costume we will take down later.

Why this matters

This matters because you will waste a week if you think you are managing a relationship. Try a blunt restart. Take a muddled draft, open a fresh message, and restate the job in four lines with one real constraint. Compare the two. The better one is usually the cleaner brief, not the longer conversation. Then look at what generative means, which is simply that the continuation is a new artifact you did not have.
