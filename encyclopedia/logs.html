Read the log

A log is a short record of inputs, outputs, and what you changed. Reading it shows the miss the happy path hid.

A log is how you see a miss that the happy path hid. You do not need a dashboard. You need a note beside the work. What you asked, in one line. What came back that mattered, especially the specific. What you changed before it left. Whether a tool was used, and what it returned. The date. If you cannot reconstruct a run from the log, you cannot trust the loop, because the next argument will be a feeling. Feelings rewrite history. The log is dull enough to resist that. I write three lines when something surprised me, and I skip the runs that were boring and correct. Boring and correct is allowed to be invisible. Surprises and near misses are not. If you log everything in detail you will stop logging. Log the sting.

Read the log once in a while on purpose, not only when you are angry. A weekly look is enough, and we will talk about that rhythm again when we talk about judgment. Here, the read has a question. What repeated. If the model keeps offering a discount you do not give, that is a brief problem. Add the ban, add a case to the tray, and confirm the next run fails closed. If a tool keeps fetching the wrong file, that is a fence problem. Narrow the file it can see. If you keep fixing the same sentence by hand, either put your sentence in the example or stop asking for that sentence. The log is a list of chores for the brief. It is not a diary about the model's personality. Personality talk will waste the review. The repeated miss will not.

Tool use deserves its own line in the log, because hands are easy to forget after the summary sounds fine. Which tool. What you thought it would do. What it actually returned. Whether you looked. I have seen people ship a number from a summary of a search they never opened. The log line 'did not open the page' is an embarrassing line and a useful one. It trains the pause. If you use an agent that takes several steps, the log should say the steps in order, even roughly. Search, then a draft, then a proposed send that you stopped. If you cannot list the steps, the agent is too opaque for the job. Opacity is a reason to take the hands away, not a reason to trust harder. Trust is a record of checked steps, not a relationship with a product.

Keep secrets out of the log if the log lives in a casual tool. The log can say 'wrong price on the recap, restored from the list' without pasting the customer's story. It can say 'tool tried to include a private attachment, run stopped' without attaching it again. A log that copies the sensitive paste is a second leak. Write the lesson, not the payload. This is the same scrubbing habit as the example folder. You are building a memory that is safe to reread, and safe for a teammate to reread if you share the job. Shared logs are how a team gets one fence instead of five private superstitions. Share the lesson. Do not share the postcard. A log that recopies the sensitive paste is a second leak with better intentions.

When a run goes badly, resist the urge to argue inside the thread and call that the record. The thread is long, defensive, and full of perfume. Extract three lines into the log and close the thread. What was the job. What failed. What you will change. If the answer is nothing, because the task should not have been given to the tool, write that. 'Do not use the helper for apologies' is a log line that becomes a fence. Fences that come from real fails are the ones people respect. Fences copied from a policy page are easy to nod at and skip. Your log is a private policy written in the language of your misses. That is the policy you will follow, because it names a miss you actually had, not a slogan you admired.

A loop you can trust is now visible. A tiny tray you rerun. Examples you have the right to use. A log of stings and changes. None of this requires a platform. It requires a note and the honesty to read it. The last module in building is what happens when the helper gets hands. Let it fetch, and you choose what to keep. Write the fence before the button exists. Name the person who can halt the run. If those three are solid, a tool is a lever. If they are vibes, a tool is a way to make a guess leave the building. We are not going to do that. Fetching inside a written fence is a lever. A guess with hands and no halt is how a draft leaves the building.

Why this matters

This matters after a run surprises you and you are about to rely on memory. Write three lines: the job, the miss, the change to the brief or the fence. Leave out the private payload. If the same miss shows up again, add it to the tiny eval instead of hoping. Then, before you give the helper a tool, write what it may fetch and who can stop it.
