Cards, looms, and instructions

A punched card stores an instruction as the presence or absence of a hole. Jacquard's loom made the method famous, and Babbage carried it into calculation.

Before a card ever touched a calculation, it touched cloth. In the early nineteenth century Joseph Marie Jacquard, working in Lyon, spread a loom that chose its pattern from a chain of punched cards. Weaving a figured silk had been a skilled setup, a matter of tying up the loom so the right threads rose at the right moment. The card moves that setup out of a single weaver's memory and into a stack you can store, copy, and put back on the machine next month. One row of the design corresponds to one card. A hole means a thread is selected. No hole means it is not. The shuttle still passes. The hands still matter. But the pattern is no longer only a habit in a body. It is an object. You can hand it to someone else, and the someone else does not have to have dreamed the flowers.

I am going to describe the mechanism only as far as the idea needs, not as a shop manual. Rods or needles meet the card. Where a hole is waiting, a piece passes through and a warp thread is lifted. Where the card is solid, that thread stays. The lift decides what the cross thread shows. Chain enough cards and a picture appears, row by row, as patient as a march. If a card is upside down, or two cards swap places, the picture grows a scar, and the loom will not tap you on the shoulder to ask whether you meant it. That indifference is the lesson hiding in the silk. A faithful machine performs the score. It does not recover your intention. You met this in the golem, when the water did not know to stop. Here the same fact is made of stiff paper. The golem's sentence and the weaver's card are relatives.

Babbage saw the relative, and he said so by the way he designed. The Analytical Engine takes the card from the textile mill and aims it at arithmetic. Operation cards say what the mill of the engine should do. Other cards say which numbers to fetch from the store. The engine, had it been built in his lifetime, would have been a loom whose flowers were results. He liked to make the kinship visible. Visitors were shown a woven silk portrait of Jacquard himself, a picture that had required a vast stack of cards, often numbered in the tens of thousands. I will not swear to a single count from memory. The point of the object is enough. A face can be a deck. If a face can be a deck, a procedure can be a deck. Hold that in your hand, imaginatively, before you let the word software float off into a cloud. Software began as something you could drop on the floor.

The card's next famous job is less poetic and just as important. By 1890 the United States census was drowning in its own counts. Herman Hollerith built tabulating machines that read punched cards, one card for a person, holes for facts, pins and counters that could total a column without a room of clerks adding by hand. It worked well enough to change the office. The company he formed sits in the lineage that later became IBM, through a merger and a rename I will not turn into a corporate saga. What you should see is the card becoming ordinary. Not a wonder in a royal demonstration. A unit of clerical truth. Entire bureaucracies learn to think in decks: sort them, merge them, punch them twice because the first clerk might have lied or slipped. The instruction is no longer a magical sentence. It is stationery. That is progress. Stationery is easier to audit than a spell, and easier to forge if nobody looks.

Spend a minute on the physical morals, because they survive the paper. A card can be duplicated, so a procedure can spread. A card can be wrong in one hole, so a tiny defect can scar a whole run. A card can be reordered, so the meaning of a process is the sequence, not the pile. A blank card is not a neutral pause if the machine treats blank as an order. When people later say that computers only do what they are told, this is the furniture they are pointing at. The telling has to be embodied. Someone punches. Someone checks. Someone decides which deck is the current one when two decks disagree. None of that is automatic just because the reading of the card is automatic. If you skip the checker, you have automated the mistake at the speed of the machine. The loom will weave the scar beautifully. Beauty of execution is not evidence that the score was right.

Follow the thread one step past the office, without jumping the nodes that are coming. A program is a descendant of this deck: instructions you can hold, copy, swap, and get wrong. For a while the deck stays outside the calculator. You feed cards in, the machine obeys, and the program is a separate object from the numbers it chews. That separation is useful. You can read the program without reading everyone's data. The stored-program idea, a little later in this module, puts the instructions into the same memory as the data, so the machine reads its job the way it reads a number. We are not there yet. Wartime rooms sit between, as context, not as the main plot. Before you leave the cards, try one concrete habit. When a tool does something strange, ask which card in the sequence was wrong, missing, or swapped. Do not begin by asking whether the mill has become a person.

Why this matters

This matters because an instruction you can hold is the ancestor of a file you can copy, and it will not forgive a swapped step. Jacquard's holes, Babbage's decks, and Hollerith's census cards are one habit in three rooms. Next, wartime machines, briefly: why calculation suddenly had budgets and secrecy, without turning the stop into a history of battles. Keep the card in mind. Colossus and its cousins are what urgency did with the idea that a machine could be set up to search. The stored program, after that, moves the deck indoors.
