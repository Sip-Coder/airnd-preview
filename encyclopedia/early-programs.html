Early programs

From the 1950s through the 1980s, AI meant specific programs: provers, chat scripts, closed worlds, and expert systems.

The Logic Theorist, from Newell, Shaw, and Simon, proves theorems out of Principia Mathematica. It is a search through possible steps, not a mystic. It matters because it shows a machine can do work that looked like reasoning if you formalize the work. A lot of early AI is that sentence in different costumes.

ELIZA is the costume students remember. Joseph Weizenbaum writes a program that answers like a bland therapist: it turns your sentence around and asks how you feel. People confide in it. Weizenbaum is rattled. He did not build a healer. He built a mirror with good timing. If you have ever felt 'seen' by a chatbot, you are in a long tradition. Notice the feeling. Then remember ELIZA had almost no world behind the curtain.

SHRDLU, Terry Winograd's project, lives in a tiny world of blocks. You can tell it to pick up a pyramid and it can, because the world is small enough to model. That is both the glory and the trap. Inside a toy room, language and action line up. Outside, the room has exceptions, slang, and a customer who changes their mind. Expert systems try a different bargain: interview a specialist, write the rules down, and let the machine apply them. MYCIN suggests treatments. XCON configures computers. They work where the rules are stable and expensive to keep. They crack when the world moves and the rulebook does not.

Take the pattern with you. Search, scripts, tiny worlds, and hand-written expertise. Each one is a real tool with a fence around it. The next node is what happens when the brochures ignore the fence and the money notices.
