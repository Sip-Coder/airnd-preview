Wartime machines, briefly

In the 1940s, codebreaking and ballistics pushed electronic and electromechanical calculation from a proposal into rooms full of working machines.

This stop is a hallway, not a book about the war. I need the hallway because otherwise the machines seem to appear because a genius woke up. They appear, in part, because states suddenly needed more arithmetic than rooms of people could finish, and they were willing to pay, to classify, and to keep secrets. Firing tables for artillery. Counts of supplies. The settings of ciphers that changed faster than a clerk could chase. Before the famous metal, the word computer was a job title. A computer was a person, often a woman, hired to compute. Teams split a calculation into columns and checked one another. Desk calculators helped. The work was still a tide. When I say programmable calculation got urgent, I mean that tide hit a wall, and the money went looking for machines that could repeat a search or a sum without getting tired and without talking about it afterward.

At Bletchley Park the secret tide had a name you may have met in a film, and a machine you may not have. Alan Turing worked on naval Enigma. The bombe, built from his logical design and improved with Gordon Welchman and others, was an electromechanical searcher. It hunted through possible settings so that human breaks could start further along. It was not a general computer, and it was not a stored program. It was a fast, narrow, brilliant sieve. I am not going to teach you how a cipher is broken. That is a different craft, and it is not the point of this encyclopedia. The point is the shape of the work: a huge space of possibilities, a procedure that tries them, a machine that tries them faster than a tired room. Turing's life, his 1936 paper, and what was done to him later belong in his own module. Here he is one reason the sieve existed. Remember to go meet the person, not only the gadget.

Colossus is the other Bletchley machine I want named, and then held in proportion. Tommy Flowers, an engineer from the telephone side of the British post office, led the building of electronic machines that helped against the Lorenz cipher, a different German system from Enigma, used for high-level traffic. The first of them was working at Bletchley in 1944. Operators changed what it searched by plugs and switches. Paper tape ran through it. It was secret for decades, which is why popular stories of computing often skip from a Victorian engine to an American room and leave a hole. Historians still argue about how far the later word programmable should stretch for Colossus, because it was a specialist searcher, not a mill you retarget at any arithmetic under the sun. I will not settle that argument with a trophy. The honest sentence is enough. Electronic calculation, set up and rerun, had become urgent enough to build, and secret enough to vanish from the public record.

Across the Atlantic the machine people could eventually see was ENIAC, built at the Moore School in Philadelphia by J. Presper Eckert, John Mauchly, and a large team, for ballistic tables the army wanted faster. It was enormous, decimal rather than binary, full of vacuum tubes, and astonishingly quick at arithmetic compared with a desk machine. It was finished as the war was ending and shown publicly in 1946. Programming it meant replugging cables and resetting switches, a physical edit of the machine's body. Among the people who learned that body, and who made it carry out real jobs, were six women whose names belong in the sentence: Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Fran Bilas, and Ruth Lichterman. I mention them because the hardware photographs tend to forget the setup. The setup was the program. If changing the job means a day with a cable in your hand, you have a powerful calculator and a painful door. That pain is the setup for the next node.

Do not let the hallway become a single corridor with one flag on it. In Germany, Konrad Zuse had already built program-controlled calculators, including an electromechanical machine in 1941 that read instructions from tape. Wartime destruction and secrecy hid a lot of that work from the people who were writing the English story. Other laboratories had their own drafts and partial builds. I am not going to score them. A beginner loses the thread as soon as the lesson becomes a medal ceremony. The thread is this. Several groups, under pressure, found ways to make a procedure run without a human doing every step. Some machines were sieves for ciphers. Some were arithmetic mills set up by hand. Some were closer to the general engine Babbage had drawn. Urgency paid for the hardware. It did not, by itself, invent the logical idea of a universal machine. Turing had published that in 1936, in peacetime mathematics. You will read it slowly in his module.

What should you carry out of a stop that refuses to be a war story? First, that speed became a national want, so odd machines got built before they got explained. Second, that secrecy edits the history you inherit. Colossus being quiet for decades is not a small footnote. It means the public met an incomplete cast and then mistook the cast for the whole play. Third, that the people in the room, clerks and programmers and engineers, were doing setup, checking, and judgment the whole time. The machines did not abolish the human computer. They moved where the human stood. Fourth, the limitation that keeps this node short on purpose. I am not teaching campaigns, generals, or the moral history of the war. I am marking why calculation grew up fast, and pointing at the bruise ENIAC made obvious: if the instructions live in the cables, changing your mind is a rebuild. The famous writeup of a cure is dated 1945. It has several parents. We go there next, and we do not hand anyone a crown.

Why this matters

This matters because the machines were answers to a pile of repetitive search and arithmetic, built in secret and in a hurry, not oracles that appeared fully explained. Do not memorize the war. Remember Colossus as a specialist, ENIAC as a fast mill you replugged, and the human computers who stood beside both. Next is the stored program: the 1945 EDVAC draft as the famous writeup, and a refusal to crown a single inventor. Turing's own life and the 1936 paper are the module after that. This hallway only points.
