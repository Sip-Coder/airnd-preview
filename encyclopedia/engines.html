Engines and Lovelace

The Analytical Engine was a proposed general-purpose machine. Ada Lovelace’s 1843 notes describe how it would be instructed, and where its pretensions stop.

Charles Babbage did not want a machine that stamped out one table of numbers and then retired. On paper, the Analytical Engine has a store for values, a mill for the arithmetic, and punched cards for the procedure. Change the cards and you change the work. He did not finish it. The idea was finished enough to matter.

In 1843 Ada Lovelace translated a paper about that engine and added notes longer than the paper. She walks through a procedure for Bernoulli numbers, which is why people call her an early programmer. She also sees that the material can be symbols, not only quantities: patterns, even music. Then she sets the brake. The engine has no pretensions to originate anything. It does what we know how to order it to perform.

Both halves are yours to keep. A computer is more than a fancy calculator, because the recipe can change. It is still not a colleague with a private muse. When a model drafts a paragraph, it rearranges what its instructions and examples make available. A promise you can stand behind is still yours.

Why this matters
