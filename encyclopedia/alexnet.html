AlexNet

AlexNet is the deep convolutional network with which Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton won the 2012 ImageNet challenge. Its top-5 error was about 15 percent, against a field near 26 percent, and the win reset the default approach to vision.

In 2012 Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton entered a deep convolutional network in the ImageNet challenge and won it by a gap the field could not shrug off. The network is called AlexNet. The number you should keep is a comparison, not a trophy. Top-5 error counts a prediction as wrong only if the correct label is missing from the model's five best guesses. AlexNet's top-5 error was about 15 percent. The rest of the field, still led by carefully engineered hand-built features, sat near 26 percent. Read that again as a beginner who does not yet love any team. The error did not tick down. It fell off a step. A shared exam is what makes that sentence possible. Without ImageNet, this would have been a demo with passionate authors. With ImageNet, it was a public change in the standings.

The network itself was a stack of learned visual filters, deep for its day, trained by backpropagation on the contest's labeled images. Several choices mattered, and none of them requires you to worship a diagram. Rectified linear units, instead of older saturating curves, let a signal stay alive as it passed through layers. Dropout randomly silenced units during training so the net could not lean on one fragile co-adaptation and call it knowledge. The training images were cropped and flipped so the set behaved as if it were larger. And the model was split across a pair of graphics processors because one card did not have the memory. I give you these as reasons the run was possible, not as a recipe to reimplement tonight. The conceptual bundle is short. A convolutional stack matched the structure of pictures. A few training tricks kept that stack learnable. The data gave it something to learn.

Graphics chips did the arithmetic, and that sentence is part of the result, not a footnote for hardware fans. A graphics processor is built to apply the same operation across a huge grid. Convolution and the giant matrix multiplies inside a net are that kind of work. Krizhevsky's training run took on the order of several days on two cards. The same experiment on the ordinary processors of the moment would have been a different project, measured in a patience most labs do not have for a single student idea. This is the pause ending in public. The method had not been waiting for a new philosophy. It had been waiting for a way to spend multiplications cheaply enough that a deep stack could see a million images before the conference deadline. When you hear 'GPUs' in this story, translate it as: the machines finally matched the method.

Be fair about the room next door. AlexNet is the turning point because of where it won and by how much, not because no other human had trained a deep convolutional net. Groups such as Dan Ciresan's, working with Jurgen Schmidhuber, had already been winning smaller vision contests with GPU-trained convolutional networks around those same years. Hand-built pipelines were still the respectable default on the big exam. What 2012 changed was the default. A margin near ten points of top-5 error, on the dataset everyone had agreed to share, is not a quirk of one leaderboard's taste. Labs that had treated nets as a 1990s disappointment started buying cards and rereading the old training papers. Graduate students felt the weather shift. I will call it a turning point in plain words because that is what the standings did. They turned the bet.

What the turning point did not do is finish seeing. About 15 percent top-5 error means the right label was still missing from the top five about fifteen times in a hundred. Top-5 is a kinder score than demanding the single best guess be correct, and the contest's images are not your warehouse, your medical archive, or a phone photo of a wet street at night. The network also inherited everything ImageNet inherited: the noun list, the crowd labels, the biases of what the web had photographed and what workers were told to call it. A win on a shared exam is a win on that exam. You are allowed to be impressed and still ask whose objects were easy, whose were muddled, and what happens when the label set ends. The people who treated AlexNet as a solved visual cortex were repeating the NETtalk mistake with a better score attached. The score was real. The solved world was not included.

Carry three sentences into the next node, which zooms out from this contest to the phrase 'deep learning.' One: error on ImageNet fell sharply, about 15 percent top-5 against a field near 26. Two: the arithmetic fit on graphics chips, so a deep stack was no longer a heroic stunt. Three: after this, 'try a deep net' became a rational first bet for seeing, and soon for hearing and more. I do not need you to memorize filter sizes. I need you to recognize a before and an after. Before, a serious vision system was mostly features an engineer drew. After, a serious vision system was mostly features a network learned, on data someone else had labeled, on chips originally sold for games. That is the turn. Everything called deep learning in the popular voice is downstream of people digesting it.

Why this matters

Read the 2012 AlexNet paper for the comparison, not for every layer width. Say the result in one breath: about 15 percent top-5 error, a field near 26, graphics chips, a convolutional net. Then look up one pre-2012 ImageNet leader so you can see what hand-built features actually were. If the gap feels concrete, you have the turning point and not just the legend. Next we name the habit the field adopted afterward: many layers, learned features, data plus chips, and a default of trying the deep net first.
