Expert systems

An expert system applies hand-written rules from a specialist to a new case, often explaining which rules fired. MYCIN and XCON showed the pattern working in fenced domains, and showed how quickly it ages when the rules go stale.

An expert system is what you get when the notebook is aimed at a specialist's job and the clerk is allowed to ask questions. Someone interviews the specialist. Someone writes the if-then rules. The program takes a new case, asks for the facts it needs, fires the rules that match, and often tells you which rules fired. That last part mattered. A recommendation you can trace is a different object from a shrug in a polished paragraph. In the 1970s and 1980s this pattern became the public face of artificial intelligence. Companies were told they could bottle a senior engineer. Some of that pitch was sales. The pattern underneath was real, and it worked in places where the job was a fenced domain: a catalog, a diagnostic checklist, a configuration whose parts either fit or do not.

MYCIN is the medical example I want you to know honestly. In the early 1970s Edward Shortliffe and colleagues at Stanford built a research system that gave advice on antibiotic choice for bacterial infections. It asked questions the way a careful clinician might: what did the culture show, where is the infection, how old is the patient, are there allergies. Then it suggested drugs. Because medicine is full of partial evidence, MYCIN attached a strength to its conclusions, a device the project called certainty factors. You can think of that as a spoken hedge made explicit. This rule helps, and it leaves room for the rest of the evidence. Several hedges could combine into a recommendation that carried its own degree of caution. The point for you is not the arithmetic. The point is that the builders knew the rules were uncertain, and they tried to keep the uncertainty visible instead of pretending the notebook was a law of nature.

MYCIN was a research system, and I need that sentence to stay intact when people talk about it as if it had been a product on a ward cart. In evaluations it compared well with human specialists on the kind of case it was built for. That was a serious result. It stayed off the casual path a doctor walks between patients. The computing of the time was awkward to put at the bedside. A hospital day is not a questionnaire on a terminal. Responsibility for a prescription sat with a person, as it should, and a research program did not dissolve that responsibility. Knowledge of infections also kept moving, which means a frozen rule base starts to age the moment the last interview ends. MYCIN taught the field that a fenced medical checklist could be written down and tested. A good score on a fenced test is a different achievement from a tool the ward will actually live with.

XCON, which began as R1, is the example from the order desk, and it earned its fame by being used. In the late 1970s John McDermott, at Carnegie Mellon, built it for Digital Equipment Corporation to configure VAX orders. A customer, or a salesperson, would ask for a computer as a pile of components. Those components do not automatically make a working machine. Power, cabinets, cables, operating choices, the unglamorous fit of part to part: get one wrong and the order ships as an expensive puzzle. R1 applied rules about what could go with what, caught incomplete orders, and added the pieces the configuration still needed. DEC put it to work. This is the success I will not let a later bust erase. Inside a product line someone had listed, a rule system saved people from a miserable family of mistakes. Use is the compliment. A demo can be arranged. A daily order desk is harder to fool.

Then the garden grew. A product line is a fenced world only for a while. DEC's catalog changed. New components arrived, old ones retired, and a rule that was true in January became a confident error in June. XCON grew to thousands of rules. The configuring still had value, and the editing became its own large chore. This is the aging I want you to picture physically. A troubleshooting guide on the wall of a shop is gold in the month you write it. Eighteen months later, three of the devices have new model numbers and one of the steps can damage the replacement board. The page did not become foolish. The world moved and the page stayed. Expert systems aged in exactly that way. The fence was real, and the fence needed repainting on the company's schedule, not on the schedule of a paper that had already been published.

Put the two stories next to each other and the pattern is simple enough to keep. MYCIN and XCON worked where the domain could be listed: organisms and drugs, or components and constraints. They were expensive to build because the listing was skilled labor. They were valuable while the list matched the world. They became risky or merely useless when the rules went stale and nobody paid for the gardening. MYCIN stayed a research system. XCON became a working industrial system and then a maintenance story. Both of them were fenced tools with a renewal cost that the excited telling often left out. When someone offers you an expert in a box, ask who wrote the rules, who is paid to edit them, and what happened the last time the world changed underneath a rule that used to be right.

If you have written a troubleshooting guide, a returns policy, or a script for a new hire, you have already met this bargain in your own handwriting. The guide is a kindness on day one. On day four hundred it misleads unless someone who still does the job is allowed to correct it. Symbolic expert systems scaled that kindness and that risk. They could apply more rules, more consistently, than a tired person with a binder. They could not want the binder to be true. Next we turn to the knowledge that never made it into the binder, the ordinary facts of a kitchen that a person carries without writing them down. That is where the fence stops being a detail and starts being the whole problem. Before you go, mark one rule in your own work that was true last year and only half true now.

Why this matters

MYCIN and XCON are the honest examples: a research advisor for antibiotics, and a working configurer of VAX orders. Each one earned its reputation inside a listed domain, and each one depended on rules that aged when the domain moved. Next is the knowledge nobody finished listing. Find one written rule in your work and name the person who would notice first if it went stale. That person is part of the system.
