Winters and springs

AI winters are stretches when funders and customers pulled back because systems could not do what the public pitch implied. Springs returned when a narrower method did a job someone could recognize and pay for.

I use winter as a word for a budget and a public mood, and I want the definition to stay dull. Weather is not the subject. Money, students, and newspaper patience move together, and twice in this history they moved away from the banner after the banner had been asked to carry more than the programs could lift. Twice the room warmed again when a narrower method started doing a job a payer could point at. If you learn the rhythm, you get harder to stampede. A bad year for a slogan is not the death of a technique. A good demo is not yet a change in how your work goes on Thursday. This overview keeps both winters in one hand, so neither one gets to pretend it was the only lesson the field was offered.

The first winter settles in the early and middle 1970s. The 1966 ALPAC report had already warned that machine translation was being described ahead of its results. Treat that report as the prelude. In the early 1970s, defense funding in the United States cooled on speech and on ambitions kin to easy translation, after narrow systems were surrounded by rhetoric that asked them to stand for something wider. In 1973 Lighthill's survey in Britain argued that the broader project was caught on a combinatorial explosion, and it gave cautious funders a respected text. Support for the speculative side shrank. What stayed easiest to defend was closer to advanced automation and to jobs with a visible product. The field did not blink out. It got quieter, and proposals got pickier about which sentences they were willing to risk.

Then a spring, and a spring is allowed to be real. Through the late 1970s and into the 1980s, expert systems gave people a story they could touch. Interview a specialist. Write the rules. Let the machine apply them, and sometimes say which rules fired. MYCIN had shown the pattern on a research problem about antibiotics. XCON had shown it on a company order desk, configuring real machines. Firms sold tools for capturing rules. Governments leaned in again. For a few years the banner was warm, and some of the warmth was earned. A fenced system that catches a bad order is not a costume. Some of the warmth was the old scheduling error in a new suit: the hope that a method which works inside a list will keep working when the list is every skill worth paying for. A spring is what hope does when it has one solid example and a market ready to generalize.

The second winter arrives in the late 1980s, and it is a business story as much as a laboratory story. The specialized computers that ran many symbolic systems were expensive. Ordinary computers were catching up, so the special hardware got harder to justify to a person with an invoice. The rule bases that had been sold as the product turned out to need constant gardening. Customers who thought they had bought a veteran engineer noticed they had also hired a maintenance duty. Firms that had grown on the boom found the market stepping back. For a while, careful sellers avoided the phrase artificial intelligence, the way a cook skips a spice that recently spoiled a meal. The technique did not become false. A checklist in a stable domain was still a checklist. What became false was the claim that the boom itself was a new era you could take to a bank.

Japan's Fifth Generation project sits in this same arc, and a later node will stay with it long enough to be fair. The overview sentence is enough for now. Announced in 1982, it was a national effort to build computers aimed at knowledge and parallel reasoning, and it wound down in the early 1990s without delivering a new era on the advertised schedule. Put it beside the expert-system boom and the shape repeats at a different altitude. Serious people, a real research program, a public promise with a calendar printed on it, and a calendar that won the argument. The work produced machines, software, and experience that traveled. The advertisement had asked for an era, and the advertisement is what other countries were reacting to. Hold the distinction between the labor and the era. That distinction is how you read every season in this module without becoming cynical or naive.

A spring that is more than a mood has a mechanism you can point at. A method that used to embarrass itself starts to do a daily job. Sometimes the change is a pile of examples large enough to matter. Sometimes it is a chip that makes a dull calculation cheap enough to repeat until it works. Sometimes it is a company willing to keep a narrow tool and throw away the cosmic sentence. The early 1980s had the narrow tool, the expert system inside its fence, and then drowned the tool in the cosmic sentence. Later turns in this history will have data and hardware as their mechanism. You need the test more than you need those chapters today. Did a specific task get reliably easier for a person with a job, inside a fence you can describe to a skeptical friend? If yes, you are looking at a spring. If the sentence is about everything, on a short calendar, you are looking at weather.

Here is how I listen, and how I hope you will. Separate a demonstrated result on a defined task from a forecast about capability in general. The first winter is the stretch when many funders learned that separation after translation and speech. The second is the stretch when many customers learned it after expert systems. The lesson did not stay learned by itself, which is why I am telling it at a table rather than assuming the culture has it filed. You will meet a pitch that sounds like the old ones. A small group, a short season, a human skill about to give way. Put both winters beside the pitch and ask for the fence, the edit, and the Tuesday after the demo. Next we stay inside the second winter long enough to see the machines and the stale rules up close. A bust has a texture. Texture is what makes the lesson hard to romanticize.

Why this matters

Count two chills, not one. The first follows overclaimed translation and speech, with Lighthill as the British marker. The second follows the expert-system boom, when special machines and stale rules met ordinary computers. A spring is real only when a defined task gets easier inside a fence you can describe. Next, the texture of that late-1980s bust. Take a current promise you have heard and underline the calendar. Then underline the actual task.
